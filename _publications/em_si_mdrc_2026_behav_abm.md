title: "*(Forthcoming)* Search Behavior and Labor Market Frictions: Implications for Modeling and Policy"
collection: publications
category: working papers
excerpt: 'Whether driven by recessions, industrial policy, or structural shifts in labor demand, economic change is inevitably accompanied by labor market frictions. These frictions vary across occupations, increasing the risk of certain groups of workers being left behind in times of economic transformation. Such challenges to labor market stability are often studied as exogenous shocks, taking the preferences and actions of workers to be fixed or negligible. However, incorporating the adaptive behavior that workers employ to cope with uncertainty is critical to understanding both aggregate labor market fluctuations and adverse labor market outcomes. Therefore, we endow a labor market model built on an occupational mobility network with empirically grounded dynamics in application effort, reservation wages, and on-the-job search intensity, drawing on U.S. micro data. The model additionally accommodates supply- and demand-side wage pressures, demographic turnover, and data-driven occupational labor demand. To identify the contribution of each mechanism, we compare six model variants of increasing complexity against national and occupationally disaggregated labor market data. Dynamic application effort and on-the-job search competition unequivocally improve model fit, capturing the cyclicality of search effort, long-term unemployment dynamics, and post-displacement wage losses across the business cycle. Reservation wages improve fit for higher-wage occupations while worsening fit for others, suggesting that reference-dependent wage preferences vary in salience across the wage distribution. Finally, we assess the labor market impacts of preferential hiring policies for long-term unemployed and new-entrant workers, finding that such policies improve employment outcomes for targeted workers and matching efficiency overall, though the magnitude of these effects is likely overestimated without the presence of behavioral frictions.'
